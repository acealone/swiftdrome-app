# Changelog

## [Unreleased]

## [0.32.1] - 2026-09-03

- An afternoon of podcasts no longer quietly fills the phone up. Episodes are streamed rather than kept, but the bytes of every one played were left behind on disk after the episode ended -- invisible, because that scratch space is deliberately left out of the storage figures, and cleared only the next time the app was started from cold. Five hour-long episodes park several hundred megabytes that way, and on a device with nothing left iOS answers by throwing the app's whole cache out at once, taking the music you had downloaded with it, to reclaim space nothing was really using. An episode's bytes are now deleted as soon as the player is done with it. Music is untouched: a song streamed to its end still lands in the cache exactly as before -- and a song the cache turns away now leaves nothing behind either.
- Swiping a queued track aside now says Entfernen on a German device, not Remove. It was the last line on the queue screen still written in English whatever language the phone was set to; the German wording had been shipped all along and simply went unused.
- Anything an hour or longer now shows its hours. A 92-minute mix, live set, opera act or podcast episode counted in raw minutes — "75:23" gone and "-92:00" left either side of the progress bar, "92:14" in the track lists, and the same figure again as the length on a song's info panel — so knowing how far in you were meant dividing by 60, while the lock screen showing the very same track disagreed with the app and called it "1:32:00". All three now count the way the lock screen does. Anything under an hour reads exactly as it did.
- Leaving the radio running no longer costs more the longer you listen. Every song played was kept in the queue for good, and the whole of it was written down again every few seconds — on the same thread that draws the progress bar and blends one track into the next — so a session carried from one launch to the next turned that into work you could eventually feel. The queue now keeps the last hundred played songs and lets go of what is behind them; what is playing, what is coming next and stepping back through what you have heard are all unchanged. Repeat All is left alone, since there the songs behind you are the first half of a loop you have asked to hear again.
- Clear at the top of the queue is big enough to hit now. It was a word about 18 points tall, and the rows it is pinned above answer a tap by jumping playback to that song -- so aiming for it and landing a few points low played something else, while landing on it dropped the songs you queued and the rest of the record you started, with nothing to undo it. The whole height of the heading now counts as pressing Clear, the same 44 points the hearts and menus answer to. It reads exactly as it did: same word, same place, same colour.

## [0.32.0] - 2026-08-30

- Podcast episodes say whether you have heard them. Every episode in a show looked the same, whether you had played it to the end, stopped halfway through it or never opened it -- and tapping one always started it again from nothing, so carrying on where you left off meant dragging the scrubber and guessing. A show now reads at a glance: an episode you finished is ticked and its title steps back, one you are partway through carries a thin bar under its title and says how much is left instead of how long it runs, and one you have not touched looks exactly as it always did. Tapping a part-heard episode picks up at the second you stopped rather than at its beginning.

  An episode counts as heard once you are within half a minute of the end, so the outro, the credits and whatever silence a show leaves at the end don't strand it at almost-finished forever. Below half a minute in, nothing is remembered at all -- a mis-tap that plays four seconds leaves the episode looking untouched, rather than filing it away as started. Where the rule gets it wrong, a long press offers Mark as Played and Mark as Unplayed, and unplayed means the episode is forgotten rather than recorded as unheard.

  Where you are in each episode follows you between devices: it is saved with your server the way your queue already is, so an episode left halfway through on the phone is halfway through on the iPad, and one finished on either is ticked on both. It is written down as you listen, on the device first, so the shows read correctly with no signal at all and the marks catch up with the server when there is one. Marking an episode unplayed while offline is the one thing that does not stick on its own -- the server is not told until you are back, and reopening the show before then can bring the tick back; marking it again once you are online settles it. Music is untouched throughout: an album's rows, and what tapping one does, are exactly as they were.

- Song Info is in every song's long press. The sheet with a song's tags -- its title, album, artist, track and disc, genre, length, format, bitrate, ReplayGain, size and whether it is on the device -- had only one way in: the ⋯ menu on the player, which means the song had to be playing first. Long-pressing a row now offers Song Info wherever songs are listed: the songs list, an album, a playlist, an artist, search results, Favourite Songs, Downloaded and the queue. The entry sits at the end of the menu, under the queue actions and keep, and opens the same sheet for the row pressed rather than for whatever happens to be playing.

- Song Info tells the year the record came out apart from the year of the pressing you have. It showed one year, labelled Released, taken from the song's own tags -- which is the edition your server holds, so a 1975 album ripped from a 2015 remaster called itself released in 2015. There are now two rows: Released, the date the record first came out, read off the album the way the Decades screen reads it, and Year, what this song's tags say. Both are shown even when they agree, so neither row turning up is itself a claim about the song. Released needs a server that reports original release dates and is left out where yours does not; on a library downloaded to the device it comes out of the index, which had been storing those dates all along without ever reading them back.

- Picking up where you left off no longer downloads the song again. Reopening the app after it has been closed puts the last song back in the player, and pressing play carried on from your position -- but the app worked out whether that song was already on the device before it had finished reading what is on the device, so a song sitting complete in the cache was treated as one that had to be fetched. In Render mode that meant the whole track downloaded a second time, over however much of your data it took, with the player standing silent behind "seeking -- waiting for the download" until the transfer reached the second you were at. A song on the device now plays from the file, instantly, the way it does at any other time; one that genuinely is not on the device is still fetched, and the wait for it is unchanged.
- And when that wait ends, the song picks up where you were rather than at its beginning. The moment the download reached your position, playback started -- with the first few seconds of the song sounding under a scrubber already sitting at 2:46, correcting itself once those seconds had played out. The engine was holding audio from the top of the track that it had decoded while it waited, and was putting the part you had asked for behind it instead of in place of it. What you hear now starts at the second the scrubber says.

- Nutty nods along to the music. With something playing, standing still is a nod in time with the song rather than the breathing idle, and the nod is now what the pet mostly does: it keeps coming back to standing there, so a walk across the mini player happens every half a minute or so instead of every few seconds. Where your server knows the song's tempo -- it does for files whose tag carried one -- the nod is timed against it, folded to whatever reads as keeping time: an ordinary song gets a nod a bar, a fast one a nod every other bar, a slow one a nod a beat. Everything else nods at a steady pace of its own. Nothing changes with the music off -- the pet breathes, wanders, sits on the edge, dives under the tab bar and answers a tap exactly as before.
- A decade is the years the records came out, not the years the files say. A song carries the year of the edition your server holds, so a 1975 album bought as a 2015 remaster filed its songs under the 2010s -- the decade of the reissue, which is not the decade anyone browsing for it has in mind. Decades now reads the original release date off the record instead, and that album sits in the 1970s where it belongs, remaster or not. This asks something of your tags: a record your server reports no original release date for belongs to no decade at all and stays off the screen, rather than falling back to the year of the pressing and landing in the wrong ten years. The dates come down with a full library sync, which the app runs once by itself after this update -- until it finishes, Decades has nothing to show.

- The library browses by decade. A Decades row sits under Genres, listing every ten years your library has music in -- the 1960s through the 2020s -- with how many songs each holds, and a decade opens the way a playlist does: a cover collage, play and shuffle, and a flat list of songs. The order is what makes it worth opening. Nobody assembled these songs, so the decade puts its best-known first -- the ones you have played most, your own ratings breaking the ties -- and a decade bigger than the screen takes says so under its title rather than pretending to be all of it. Podcast episodes stay out of it: a weekly show would otherwise outnumber every record of its decade. Songs your server reports no year for belong to no decade and are left out. It is worked out from the downloaded library rather than asked of the server, which has no notion of a decade at all, so the screen offers to download the library first if that has never finished.

- The lock screen skips by fifteen seconds while a podcast is playing. The two buttons either side of play are now -15s and +15s for an episode, instead of previous and next track: on a phone in a pocket the step you want is the one back over a line you missed, and dragging the scrubber blind is an awkward way to ask for it. Songs are unchanged -- an album still gets previous and next track, where stepping fifteen seconds at a time would be no way to move through a record. The player screen inside the app is unchanged too, and still carries next and previous episode in its outer buttons; for the length of an episode, a headset's double-press no longer skips episodes, because those buttons are what the lock screen gives up to show the fifteen-second ones.

- The lock screen has a heart. The song playing can be starred, or unstarred, without unlocking the phone: the button sits among the playback controls wherever the system makes room for one -- the lock screen, Control Centre, CarPlay -- and draws filled for a song that is already a favourite. It is the same heart as the one on the player screen, so a song loved from the car is starred on the server and filled in on every screen that draws a heart, and one loved on a screen fills the lock screen's. There is no thumbs-down beside it: your library has a star and no opposite of one.

- Favourite Songs lists its songs the way every other list of songs does. The heart at the end of each row is gone: on the one screen where every row is a favourite already, it only ever said what the screen's own title said, and it took the width that the rest of the app gives to the download badge and the running time. Each row now shows whether the song is on the device -- cached, kept, or still arriving -- and how long it runs, in the same columns as the songs list, an album, a playlist and an artist. Swiping a row right plays the song next, swiping it left plays it last, and a long press opens the same menu those screens offer, unstarring included; that is where unstarring a song already lived everywhere else, and the row stays put until the list is next loaded, so a song unstarred by accident can be starred straight back.

- Pressing pause and play while a song is buffering no longer starts it over. On a link the song is arriving over, playback holds while the download catches up -- and a press of pause and play during that hold cost the listener their place twice over. A hold ended by pausing was counted as a second hold against the connection, which is the point at which the app gives the song up and asks the server for it again at a lower bitrate, from the top of the file; and where the song had been picked up part way through, the engine was still holding the very beginning of the track, so play sounded 0:00 under a scrubber sitting at 2:30. Pause now keeps the position and play carries on waiting for that same second to arrive, playing from there the moment it does. Your own thumb is no longer read as evidence that the connection is failing, and the position a pause writes down -- for the next launch, and for the other devices signed in -- is where you actually are rather than the start of the song.

- Tapping the Favourite Podcasts heading on the home screen opens the shows instead of failing. It asked the server for a list of "starred podcasts", which is not a thing any server has -- podcasts are picked out of your starred records by the app itself -- so the heading only ever answered with an error. Both favourites headings now open what their shelf shows: shows under Favourite Podcasts, records under Favourites, where the full list had previously mixed the two together. A show the server split into several records by guest still opens as the one show, with all its episodes, and unhearting one leaves the list straight away.

- The progress bar on Now Playing can be used with VoiceOver. It was drawn rather than built from a control and only ever answered a drag, which VoiceOver does not perform, so it could not be focused and there was no way at all to move inside a track from within the app — the lock screen's scrubber was the only one a VoiceOver listener could reach. It now takes focus, says where you are and how long the track runs, and a swipe up or down moves the playhead by fifteen seconds, or by a twentieth of the track on something long enough that fifteen seconds would mean a hundred swipes. The times are spoken as minutes and seconds rather than read out as two bare numbers.
- The controls on Now Playing that were still unnamed now say what they are, in your own language: play and pause, the previous and next buttons, the heart, the ⋯ menu and the sleep timer. Play and pause and the heart say which of the two they will do, so what you hear matches the symbol.
- Your favourites are in the order you liked them, newest first. Favourite Songs, Albums, Artists and Podcasts, and both favourites shelves on the home screen, were alphabetical -- so the record you hearted this morning sat wherever its name put it, and finding it again meant scrolling past everything you liked years ago. Each list now starts with the most recent star and works backwards, and a record you heart goes straight to the top of Favourite Albums rather than waiting for the next sync. Where a server reports no dates for its stars at all, the lists stay alphabetical as before. The dates come down with the next library sync, so the new order appears once that has run.

- The hearts are big enough to hit now. Starring a song was a bare glyph barely 18 points across -- less than a fifth of what Apple asks a control to be -- so aiming at one while walking, or in the car, missed as often as it landed, and a miss landed on the row behind the glyph instead. The heart and the ⋯ menu on the player, and the heart, keep and ⋯ menu on an album, playlist or artist, all answer a full 44 points around the glyph now. Nothing looks any different: the symbols are the same size, in the same places -- there is simply more of the screen that counts as pressing them.

- The music can be told to stop at the end of the record. Until now the queue always carried on: when the album, playlist or artist you put on ran out, the app gathered songs like it and played them, and the only ways to reach an end were to sit there and press pause or to guess the length with a sleep timer. Open the queue and there is now an ∞ switch at the top of it, next to the heart. Tapped off, the songs the app had gathered are dropped and no more are fetched, so a record, a mix or a long live set ends where its last song does -- the Continuous Queue heading stays behind with a line saying playback stops when Up Next ends, and tapping ∞ again finds more like whatever is playing and fills the queue straight back up. The switch is lit while it is on, remembered between launches, and left alone it is on, so nothing changes for anyone who wants the music to keep going.


## [0.31.0] - 2026-08-30

- Search results are now in the order you would read them. Until now each list came back in whatever order it was matched in -- the offline index and the server rank differently, and neither ranks the way a list is read, so the rows also reshuffled the moment the server's answer replaced the index's. Every answer is now put in one order: the name you typed exactly, then names that start with it, then names with the word inside them, and a match on a song's own title always ahead of a match on the artist behind it. Songs that match equally well -- every song by an artist whose name you typed -- are ordered by how often you have played them and how you rated them, so an artist's best-known songs come first, the way they do on the artist screen. Artists matching equally well are ordered by how much of them your library holds. Where there is nothing to tell two rows apart they keep the order they arrived in. Play counts have to be gathered before they can order anything, so the first launch after this update runs a full library sync.

- The Top tab of search shows five of each kind instead of everything at once. It was one long run of artists, then albums, then songs, so the songs were most of a scroll away from a search that had found all three. Each section now stops at five rows, and the See All in its heading opens the full list as it always did. Tapping the fifth song still plays into the sixth and the rest -- what is playing is every match, not only the rows on screen.

- Tapping an artist in search no longer opens an empty screen. Search finds artists your library does not file under their own name -- somebody credited on a track rather than on a record -- and their screen came up blank: no name across the banner, no albums, nothing to play. The name from the row you tapped is now written across the banner immediately, the artist is looked up on the server, and where the server has no record of its own for them the screen falls back to the albums their songs appear on and to their most-played songs. It fills in offline too, from what the library already holds.

- The pet goes with the player now. Opening the full-screen player used to make it vanish off the mini player; it is instead thrown up off the row ahead of the rising player, out through the top of the screen, and comes back down head-first behind the album cover. It spends the player's time up there the way it spends a mini player the tab bar has folded away: every so often its head comes up over the top edge of the cover, somewhere different each time, looks around for a moment and ducks back behind it. Closing the player throws it back out onto the mini player. It stays out of sight while the cover is the small thumbnail beside the titles, on the queue and lyrics screens.

- Up Next can be emptied in one go. The heading over it now carries a Clear button, which drops every song waiting there -- the ones you queued by hand and the rest of the record you started -- without touching the song playing or the Auto Queue underneath, so the music carries on into what the app has gathered instead of stopping. The songs cleared this way are not held against the automatic picks: any of them may be gathered again later, unlike a song you remove one at a time by swiping it away.

- Shelves of covers now settle on a cover instead of stopping wherever your finger left them. Every row you scroll sideways -- the home screen's shelves and its artists, an artist's albums and the artists like them, and your kept records -- catches the nearest cover as it slows and squares it against the left edge of the row, so a flick lands on whole covers rather than halves. The gap at the edge of the screen is the same for every cover in a shelf, where before only the first one had it.

## [0.30.0] - 2026-08-30

- The repeat button has a fourth setting: the songs you queued by hand, round and round among themselves. Press it once more from Repeat Track and the loop is just your own queue -- a song that has played goes to the back of it instead of being spent, so three queued songs are heard as a loop of three and the record underneath waits where it stood. It reads as the repeat symbol with a person standing where Repeat Track puts its "1". Skipping walks the loop rather than leaving it, tapping a song further down the queue rotates the loop to it instead of throwing away what it stepped over, and turning the setting off simply plays out what is left and carries on with the record. With nothing queued by hand there is no loop, and it behaves exactly like repeat being off -- queueing a song then starts the loop without touching the button again. The loop survives closing the app.

- The queue now says which songs you put there. Up Next lists the songs you queued and the rest of the record you started as one run of rows, because that is the order they will be heard in -- but the two were indistinguishable, and only the new repeat setting makes the difference audible. The ones you queued yourself now carry a small mark on the corner of their artwork. Dragging a song from the record up above your queued ones adopts it into your queue, and the mark follows.

- Holding down a cover now opens a menu, everywhere covers are shown: the home screen's shelves, albums, playlists, podcasts, genres, favourites, downloads and kept records. Play it, shuffle it, put it next in the queue or last, star it, keep it on the device, or walk to the artist who made it. A record credited to several artists asks which one you meant rather than guessing. On a podcast show the server has split into several entries, the menu acts on the whole show, the way opening it does.

- The player can now tell you about the song it is playing, and take you to where it came from. The menu on Now Playing has three new entries: Song Info opens a sheet with the title, album and artist, the track and disc, the year and genre, the length, the format and bitrate, its ReplayGain and peak, the file size, whether the song is kept, cached or not downloaded at all, and the song's ID. Go to Album and Go to Artist leave the player and land on that screen in the Library tab. Each trip lands on one screen with the Library behind it, rather than piling up every album you have looked up this session.

- The play button on an album, playlist or artist is a pause button while that record is the one playing. It was always a play glyph, so the one big button on the screen said "play" at a record that was already sounding, and pressing it started it again from the top. It now pauses, and turns back into a play button that resumes. A record that is merely paused still shows a play button, and so does one that isn't the record playing -- pressing that still starts it.

- Checking the connection from Settings now recovers a bad link on the spot. When the app judges a connection too slow to stream it downloads every song before playing it, and it stays that way until the link proves otherwise -- which deliberately takes a run of fast readings over at least half a minute, so a connection that merely stutters cannot talk its way out of it while nobody is watching. Somebody who has just walked out of the tunnel is watching, and had no way to say so: the check on the server card only pinged, and a ping says the server answered, not that the link can carry music. It now measures as well as pings, and one measurement that clears what streaming needs with room to spare ends the verdict there and then -- the dot goes green and the next song is streamed instead of downloaded first. The quality the app asks for is restored with it, where the measurement shows the connection can carry what Settings asks for; otherwise it climbs back as it did before. A check made on a link that is still slow changes nothing, and one made while a song is downloading reads the speed off that download rather than competing with it for the connection.

- Render is now what the app plays on, and is marked beta rather than alpha. It decodes songs itself, which is what makes one track follow another with genuinely nothing in between and makes dragging the progress bar land where you dropped it. Anyone who has already chosen an engine keeps their choice -- this is what a fresh install starts on. Anything still arriving over the network plays on System as before, and the switch is now the first thing in Playback settings if you want to go back.

- Song lengths line up down a list again. The cache badge sits immediately before the length on every track row, so a shorter length pulled the badge along with it: one ten-minute song on an album of four-minute ones left that row's badge a character out of line with all the others. The length now sits in a column wide enough for two-digit minutes, so the badges stay in a straight line. A long podcast episode still shows its full length, and the column grows with the text size.

- Song titles in a list now run right up to the cache badge before they are cut short. Every track row left a gap of about a finger's width between the end of the title and the badge, which was nothing but spacing counted twice -- so titles were being truncated with empty room to their right. That room now belongs to the title, on every list that shows a badge: albums, playlists, an artist's songs and top songs, the songs list, downloads, favourites, the queue and search results. Long titles show around two more characters, and the badges and lengths sit where they did.

## [0.29.0] - 2026-08-30

- AAC can no longer be picked as the streaming or download format. It has been greyed out in Settings since it was withdrawn -- a server encoding AAC as it sends cannot say how much silence it put at the front of each track, so the gap between songs is audible and nothing in the app can remove it -- but the row stayed tappable and the setting could still be chosen. It is now genuinely unpickable, on all three rows that offer a format. The row itself stays in the menu, greyed, so there is still an answer on screen to "where did AAC go?", and anyone whose setting was already AAC keeps it rather than having it changed under them.
- A song is no longer fetched all over again when the connection turns bad late in it. When the app decides a connection can no longer carry a stream it moves the song onto a fresh, smaller download -- which is right while the song still needs the connection, and pointless once it doesn't. A song with the rest of itself already on the device was being given up on anyway: the music stopped, several seconds of silence followed, and the app spent them re-fetching bytes it was throwing away as it asked for them. Observed with 78% of a song downloaded and the playhead at 1:41 of 3:00 -- every second still to play was already there. It now plays on from what it has, and the verdict about the connection is left to apply to whatever is fetched next. A song that genuinely is still waiting on the connection is moved as before. Render mode already worked this way; this brings the ordinary player in line with it.
- Seeking a song that is still arriving now costs you nothing with the app's own playback engine turned off, too. Until now this only worked in Render mode: everywhere else, dragging through a song you were half-way through downloading threw that download away and started fetching the song again from somewhere else -- so a few seeks in a row could fetch the same song three times over and leave you with none of it. The bytes already on the device are now played straight out of the file being written, exactly as Render mode does: nothing is asked of the server, the bar keeps filling, and the song still lands in your downloads when it finishes. A jump to a part that has not arrived yet still asks the server, as before. This also fixes seeking in an Ogg that is still downloading -- the one container iOS cannot seek by itself, which until now was only exact once the whole file was on the device -- and picking a song back up part way through, after a stumble or a slow connection, now lands on the second it was asked for instead of dropping short of it and running out of audio mid-song. Podcast episodes are deliberately unchanged.
- MP3 joins that: a song being streamed as MP3 is now seekable out of what has arrived, like FLAC and Opus, instead of asking the server. And every one of the three now lands on the second you asked for rather than at the frame or page boundary just before it -- the fraction in between is decoded and dropped, which is exact by counting where cutting the file can only ever be exact to a boundary. Seeking inside a song the app had already been asked to re-fetch from part way in works too: that file holds the song from the point it was asked for, and jumping about within it no longer goes back to the server either.
- The music comes back after the system's audio restarts. iOS occasionally restarts the part of the system that produces sound, which takes every player the app holds with it, and the app answers by building new ones and putting the song back where you were. That part worked. But one restart is reported to the app several times over, and the app took the repeats for a device resetting again and again -- something it deliberately refuses to chase, because chasing it is a loop. Refusing wrote a playback error to the screen and marked the song paused, and it did that to the very player the recovery had just built and was still filling. The song then finished loading, moved itself to the second you had been listening at, and sat there in silence behind an error message, with the play button showing. The repeats are now recognised as the same restart and dropped, so the recovery is left alone to finish, and a restart that genuinely happens again straight away is still given up on as it was before. The app also waits for the system to be ready before rebuilding, rather than rebuilding onto audio that is still away and having the attempt die a second later; and the song picks up at the second you were listening at, on both the ordinary player and in Render mode, instead of starting again from the top.

- A song still arriving keeps what it had already downloaded when the system's audio restarts. The restart takes the app's players with it, but it does not touch the download or the part of the file already on the device -- and the app was throwing away both and asking for the song again from its first byte, over the slow connection that was the reason for fetching it ahead of playing in the first place. That was half a minute of silence spent re-fetching bytes the app already had, immediately after a stumble you had already heard. The download now carries on through the restart and the sound resumes from the part already on the device, normally within a second or two of the recovery.

- A song no longer goes quiet for minutes when the connection turns bad in the middle of it. When the app judges a connection too slow to stream, it downloads each song before playing it -- but the song already playing was exempt in exactly the case that matters most. It is normal for playback to pause for a moment while it waits for more audio to arrive, and that pause is usually the very thing that makes the app decide the connection is bad; a song paused that way was read as having nothing to move, so it carried on being fetched as the server's own untouched file. On one connection that meant pulling an 843 kbps original over a link carrying 90 KB/s against the 158 KB/s it needed: eighteen seconds of music, then a hundred seconds of silence and still counting when the log was taken. A song paused for more audio is now moved onto a download at a size the connection can carry, like any other. Two further ways that silence could go on for ever are closed with it. A pause for more audio that has lasted ten seconds while the connection measures below what the song needs now gives up and fetches the rest of the track instead of waiting. And silence while bytes are still arriving, but arriving too slowly to play, now counts as the stream having failed -- the app used to give up only when nothing at all was arriving, which a slow trickle never is, so the one case where waiting could not possibly help was the one case it waited through. Render mode already did all three and is unchanged.

- A song that fails on a slow connection now teaches the app what to ask for next. Outside Render mode, when a stream died mid-song the app remembered that the connection was in trouble but not what quality it had failed at -- so the next song was fetched ahead at exactly the bitrate that had just proved too much, and failed the same way. The two things a failure teaches were being kept apart: whether to download a song before playing it, and what size to ask for. It now learns both, as Render mode already did.

- A song that stops playing before its end is given up on sooner. When the audio runs out but the player keeps its clock moving, the app waits a few seconds and then moves on rather than sitting in silence. It used to require the player to agree the song was over, and the player's idea of how long a song is comes from dividing its size by a bitrate it assumes -- which for these files runs about seven per cent long. One song ended at 3:27 and the app waited until 3:48 to accept it, twenty-one seconds of nothing. Where the app has counted the length off the audio itself, which it now does once a song has fully arrived, it no longer waits for the player to catch up, and it waits a second rather than five before accepting the song is over -- a counted length is exact, so there is nothing for the longer wait to allow for. The same song is now given up on at 3:28 rather than 3:48. Where the length is only what the server said, the old caution stands.

- The progress bar stays where you are while a song is being fetched again. When a connection drops mid-song the app downloads the track afresh and puts playback back where the sound stopped -- but the download starts at the beginning of the file, so for a while the player really is at 0:00, and the bar followed it there and then snapped back once the audio caught up. It now holds your position for the whole of that wait, from the moment the fetch begins until the sound returns to where you were. - A song that runs out of downloaded audio no longer skips itself. On a slow connection playback can reach the point the download has got to. Outside Render mode nothing was watching for that: the player read to the end of what had arrived, treated it as the end of the song, and moved on to the next one -- one track was lost at 1:02 of 1:38 this way. Two things now happen a second before that edge, depending on why the audio is about to stop. How much audio is left is reckoned from the bytes that have arrived, which is an estimate -- what the player itself reports about the audio it holds turned out to be unusable for this, reading as nothing at the moment a song starts and as almost the whole song a second later. If the connection has died and nothing is fetching the rest, the song is asked for again from exactly where you are -- before the sound stops, so the track is never mistaken for one that has finished and the queue never moves on. The engine reports a song as finished the moment its last buffer has been played, and that report was read as the end of the queue even mid-blend, so the fade lost a race against the very thing it was waiting for. The end of the outgoing song is now what *completes* the blend, exactly as it already did on the system player, so the overlap runs to its end and the next song carries on from where the fade left it.
- A song you removed from Up Next is no longer played in its place. Once the app is within a few seconds of a song's end it has already written the next one into the engine, and that arrangement was invisible to the queue -- so removing the songs that followed left the app running on into one of them while the player showed something else entirely, or skipping past the song that should have come next. Every queue edit now asks the engine what it has lined up, the app can no longer claim an overlap the engine turned down, and a boundary the two disagree about is reloaded from the queue rather than guessed at.
- A blend the render engine could not go through with no longer fades the music into silence. Where the app asked for an overlap the engine then had to refuse -- because it was already committed to joining the next song on, or because the incoming song's file would not open -- the fade ran its curve anyway against a song that was never started, and the app came out the far side showing a song it wasn't playing. The engine now says whether it has taken a blend on, and says so again if one dies before it sounds; either answer leaves the boundary alone and the song playing untouched.
- The render engine crossfades into songs that aren't on the device yet. It could only blend into a complete file, which meant no overlap at all the first time through a queue -- the songs the app gathers for you and anything you just queued are, by definition, not downloaded yet, so those were exactly the boundaries that fell flat. The song coming in is now fetched and decoded as it arrives, the same way the engine already streams a song you start. It waits for enough of the song to have landed before the fade begins, and the overlap is measured against what's left of the song playing, so one that starts late is simply a shorter overlap rather than a wrong one -- and if nothing has arrived by the time the song ends, the boundary is left plain instead of fading into silence. The song coming next is fetched by the transfer that is going to play it, started when the song before it starts rather than at the boundary -- so the overlap usually opens on music that has been arriving for minutes, nothing is fetched twice, and that same transfer becomes the copy in your downloads when it finishes. A poor connection no longer means no overlap either: the song is asked for at the smaller version that connection has actually been measured carrying, the same judgement playback itself makes. The one thing it deliberately won't do is open a second stream while the song you're listening to is itself still arriving -- that one has the better claim on the connection. Paused, the next song is fetched the ordinary way instead, so a download still finishes while the app is in the background.
- Jumping back into a song you have already heard part of works with no connection at all. The app reads those seconds off the file it collected while the song was playing -- but the moment the transfer behind that file stopped, which is exactly what a dropped connection is, it treated the file as though it were empty and asked the server for the jump instead: the one thing that cannot work when there is no connection. It now reads what arrived whatever became of the transfer, so a jump backwards plays straight away, from the device, offline. The part of a song that arrived before the connection went bad is also no longer thrown away when the app asks for the rest at a smaller bitrate -- that file is kept for as long as the song is playing, and a jump back into it picks up from there.
- A song that finished downloading while it was playing is no longer stopped near its end to be asked for all over again. On a slow connection a song plays as it arrives, and if the whole file lands separately -- you pressed Download, or the app fetched it ahead of the playhead -- playback now moves onto that copy the moment the stream it started on falters, picking up at the same second with nothing to wait for. Every recovery used to ask the server instead, at a smaller bitrate each time, for a song that was already on the device: the music stopped a few seconds from the end and said "Buffering" while a complete copy of it sat in your downloads. The same now goes for a connection the app has just judged unreliable -- a song it can play from the device is played from the device rather than left on a stream over a link that is failing.

## [0.8.0] - 2026-08-12

- Songs streamed as Opus can be downloaded again. Every Opus download was being thrown away the moment it finished, on the grounds that it had arrived incomplete — and it hadn't: iOS works an Opus file's length out from an assumed bitrate rather than reading the length the file states, and it was wrong by up to half a minute on a three-and-a-half-minute song. Nothing reached the device, so nothing could be played offline, and the app kept trying: the same song was fetched again a second later, and again, for as long as it was in the queue. An Opus file is now measured by what it says about itself, exact to the sample, and the check that rejects a genuinely truncated download is the file's own account of whether it ended.
- Scrubbing a song streamed as Opus now lands where you drop it. A server transcodes as it sends, so the stream has no length and no way to be asked for a position part way through — a scrub either settled back where playback already was or never arrived. The app now asks the server to start the song where you dropped the scrubber, which it can do because it is reading its own file, and covers the last fraction of a second itself. Landing is exact. Servers that don't support this are detected rather than assumed, and scrubbing behaves as it did before on them.
- The length shown for an Opus song is the song's, not an estimate. The progress bar could be built on a length several percent short of the audio — a three-minute song reported as two-fifty — which put every position, the remaining time and the end of the track out with it.
- A download that fails is no longer retried immediately, over and over. One that couldn't be fetched was being started again the instant it failed, roughly once a second for as long as the song stayed in the queue, and each attempt pulled the whole song down before being discarded. A failed download now waits five minutes, and the songs behind it in the queue are fetched in the meantime instead of being held up behind it.
- Scrubbing a lossless song now lands where you drop it. Dragging to 2:50 in a three-minute FLAC could start the audio nearly a minute earlier while the progress bar and the remaining time both insisted you were where you had asked to be — iOS works a FLAC's position out from an assumed bitrate instead of reading the seek table the file carries, so the further into a song you reached the further short it landed, and it reported the position you asked for either way. Downloaded songs are now kept in a container iOS can seek exactly: still lossless, still the same audio, and about the same size on disk. A song that is still streaming is fetched from the byte your seek points at, which lands within a tenth of a second. Nothing on the server is touched, re-encoded or renamed — this happens on the device, to the app's own copy.
- Reopening the app returns to where you left a lossless song, rather than to wherever the seek happened to land. The check meant to catch a restored position that missed was trusting a clock that reports the position it was asked for, so it never found anything to correct.
- A download that arrives incomplete no longer becomes a permanently broken song. Two ways a bad file used to end up in the cache looking like a good one: a server answering with an error page instead of the track, stored as if it were music, and a connection closing part-way through a transfer the app had no length to check it against. Either one is poison once cached — a half-finished FLAC still claims the full length in its header, so it plays out early, skips, or lands somewhere else every time you scrub. Downloads are now checked against the file size and length the server reports for the track, and anything short is discarded instead of kept.
- The app has a real icon. Installed builds used to sit on the home screen as a blank placeholder, because nothing in the build carried an icon at all; SwiftDrome now ships its own mark — two triangles for the outgoing and incoming track, with the overlap where both are audible drawn in its own lighter colour. The same mark greets you on the login screen in place of the stock music symbol, and the AltStore source now shows it as the app's icon and header rather than two broken images.

## [0.4.0] - 2026-08-05

### One track into the next

- Tracks now crossfade: the next one starts before the current one ends, with the outgoing track fading out as the incoming one fades in. The overlap is 6 seconds by default and can be set anywhere from 0 to 12, or turned off, on the new Playback screen in Settings.
- Consecutive album tracks are joined gaplessly instead of blended, so records mastered as one continuous piece finally play as one. Only a genuine continuation is joined — track 1 into track 3 was never meant to butt together, so it gets the crossfade like any other transition, as does a track the server sends no number for.
- A gapless join needs the server's original file or an Opus stream: any other transcode on the way out strips the information a seamless join depends on. Wi-Fi asks for the original file by default, so that's where it applies unless you change it.
- Playback no longer runs out. The queue keeps itself stocked with ten tracks similar to what you're listening to, topped up as they play, so a single song can carry on indefinitely. Where a server has no similar tracks to offer, it falls back to other songs by the artists you're playing, drawn from a random album of theirs rather than the same few tracks every time. Starting from an album still queues the rest of that album first.

- The screen has been rebuilt around the crossfade, which you can now see happening: the cover art of the track coming in dissolves across the one going out, the background colour turns over with it, and a moving indicator in the metadata line tracks the blend. - Swiping a track away removes it, on the tracks still to come rather than on ones already played, and the row goes with it. A track you remove from the continuous queue is not offered again the next time the queue tops itself up.
- Your queue survives closing the app. Reopening restores what was playing along with everything queued behind it, what had already been played, and the tracks gathered to follow on.

### Streaming and storage

- Streaming quality is now set per connection. Wi-Fi and cellular each choose what to ask the server for — the original file, or Opus, AAC or MP3 — and a bitrate from 96 up to 320 kbps when transcoding. A line on the same screen says what all of it adds up to on the connection you're on right now.
- Seeking is immediate on Wi-Fi: the track is quietly downloaded while it plays, so dragging the progress bar has the whole song to work with. On cellular, and anywhere Low Data Mode is on, nothing is downloaded speculatively — seeking there fetches the track at the moment you ask for it, showing "Buffering to seek…" for the moment that takes, after which every later seek in that track is instant.
- The most recently played tracks are kept on the device, so replaying a song or skipping back to it plays from local storage instead of downloading it again.
